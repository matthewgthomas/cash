# Evidence on payment timing and recipient selection for humanitarian cash assistance

This page summarises the evidence underlying the questions and logic in my [Cash Assistance Design Helper](https://matthewgthomas.co.uk/cash/). The helper tool is intended to provoke a structured discussion to support decisions about:

1. How cash should be paid: whether in one lump sum, several smaller payments, or a bigger payment first followed by smaller ones.
2. How recipients should be chosen: whether by the organisation disbursing the cash, by the community for onward distribution, or a hybrid.

This is not a scientific formula giving you the correct answer. The underlying model is directionally correct, based on the available evidence, but should not be considered predictive of individual, household or community behaviours or outcomes.

## Timing of cash payments

Overall, the evidence suggests that larger early payments help most when people need enough money in one go to do something meaningful. This might include agricultural inputs, productive assets, or pre-emptive protection of livestock/shelter. Monthly payments are good for smoothy out volatility and insecurity. Over the medium-to-longer term, both approaches seem equally effective. It's important to note that results may be context-sensitive rather than universal.

One [study in Democratic Republic of Congo](https://odihpn.org/en/publication/cash-transfers-vouchers-conflict-affected-households-democratic-republic-congo/) found that receiving a lump sum rather than spread-out payments did not materially affect people's outcomes. Recipients preferred the lump sum payments and they were operationally simpler to deliver.

In [Ghana](https://wfp-evaluation.medium.com/lump-sum-or-monthly-understanding-cash-transfer-timing-91c43e17ccf5), farmers were more likely to invest lump-sum payments in productive assets such as bulk-buying fertiliser, which translated into larger harvests and higher crop sales. Lump-sum payments also improved food security in the short-term, although smaller monthly payments were equally as effective over the longer-term.

Similarly, in [the Philippines](https://mcdl.mercycorps.org/TabangKOBeyondImmediateNeedsSummaryEval.pdf), recipients of lump-sum payments following Typhoon Haiyan were more likely to invest in productive assets such as livestock, which could build longer-term resilience among households.

In [Kenya](https://haushofer.ne.su.se/publications/Haushofer_Shapiro_UCT_QJE_2016.pdf), lump-sum payments were more likely to be spent on durable assets, whereas monthly payments were more likely to improve food security.

In [Niger](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6175357/), receiving payments earlier and over a longer period improve short-term coping but did not materially improve long-term outcomes compared to a shorter schedule of payments.

For humanitarian actors disbursing cash assistance, single payments are simpler to administer and [reduce delivery costs](https://odihpn.org/en/publication/cash-transfers-vouchers-conflict-affected-households-democratic-republic-congo/).

## Choosing recipients

The evidence base is much thinner on the relative benefits of agency-led vs community-led targeting of cash assistance.

Community involvement can improve legitimacy, reduce some complaints of exclusion, and sometimes increase ownership/fairness, according to a seminal [review of humanitarian cash assistance](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.4073/csr.2017.17#cl2013001035-bib-0102). But it can also create tension, be manipulated by local elites, and fail if complaints have to go through the same local powerholders who make the distribution decisions. However, these patterns are likely to be context-specific rather than generalisable.

A [synthesis of evaluations](https://www.3ieimpact.org/sites/default/files/2019-01/wp30-cdd_0.pdf) of community-driven development programmes found that community-managed grants can improve small-scale infrastructure but did not meaningfully improve people's health or education. The participation of minoritised groups in decision-making needs to be a requirement, not just a target.

Community-led targeting of cash assistance can [increase perceived legitimacy and satisfaction](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.102.4.1206), though might not identify the poorest households. (Interestingly, this is due to different ways researchers/NGOs and locals understand poverty.)

Other studies in [Mexico](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.99.1.486) and [Kenya](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26600/w26600.pdf) found that households who do not receive cash transfers themselves can still benefit, for example through receiving gifts and loans from households that were eligible.

Neither approach consistently and predictably improves outcomes, so **defaulting to a hybrid model** might be better unless expertise and experience (both yours and those of local actors and potential recipients) suggest otherwise. If you're selecting recipients yourself, **be transparent** in how you make your decisions.
